Parallels vs VMware Fusion Black Friday 2026: Which Mac VM Wins?

Two years ago, this comparison was easy to frame: Parallels costs money, VMware Fusion costs money, pick the one whose feature list matches your workflow. Then Broadcom made VMware Fusion free for personal use, and in late 2025 extended that to commercial use too. That single decision rearranged the entire Mac virtualization market.

So here we are in November 2026, a couple of weeks out from Black Friday (November 27) and Cyber Monday (November 30), and the question is no longer "which one is better." It's "is Parallels still worth paying for when the other guy is free?"

Short answer: sometimes yes, often no. Long answer is below.

The thing that changed everything

VMware Fusion Pro is now free — for personal use and for commercial use, no license key, no per-seat math. Broadcom announced the commercial expansion in November 2025 alongside the Fusion 25H2 release, and it's held through 2026. You download it from Broadcom's support portal, you register an account, and you run VMs.

That's not a small thing. It used to be $199 for Fusion Pro. Now it's zero dollars, and it still runs ARM Linux beautifully, still supports snapshots, still has a CLI, and still lets you build custom virtual networks for testing distributed systems.

Parallels, meanwhile, is still a paid product. Standard Edition runs $99.99/year on subscription or about $129.99 for a perpetual license. Pro Edition is $119.99/year (or $199.99 perpetual). Business is $149.99/year per seat. Students get in around $49.99/year.

Which means the real Black Friday question for 2026 is whether a Parallels discount — historically in the 25–30% range, occasionally deeper on the subscription tier — is enough to justify paying anything at all.

Feature breakdown: where the money actually goes

Windows 11 on Apple Silicon

Both apps run ARM versions of Windows 11 on M-series Macs. Neither one can run x86 Windows on Apple Silicon — that's an Apple hardware constraint, not a vendor limitation, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling you something.

The difference is in how you get there. Parallels detects that you want Windows, downloads the Windows 11 ARM image from Microsoft, and walks you through the install with a Microsoft account login. Ten minutes, maybe fifteen, and you're in a desktop. It also sells you a Windows license during setup if you don't have one, which is convenient even if you'd rather buy elsewhere.
