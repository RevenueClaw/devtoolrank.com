Norton VPN vs OrionVPN: Which VPN Is Worth Your Money in 2026?

These two show up in the same search results for a reason, but they're not really the same kind of product. Norton Secure VPN is a security-industry veteran's VPN, sold mostly as a bundle add-on and backed by a third-party audit. OrionVPN is a smaller, budget-first provider that undercuts almost everyone on price and asks you to take a fair amount on faith.

That difference matters more than any spec sheet. So instead of lining up bullet points and declaring a tie, let's look at what each one actually does well, where each one falls short, and which one deserves your card details.

The short version: Norton is the safer purchase for most people. OrionVPN is cheaper and faster to set up, but its privacy claims haven't been independently verified, and that's a real cost even if it doesn't show up on the invoice.

What you're actually buying

Norton Secure VPN

Norton Secure VPN comes from Gen Digital, the company formed when NortonLifeLock merged with Avast. It's sold two ways: as a standalone VPN subscription, or bundled inside Norton 360 plans (Deluxe, Premium, and so on), where it sits alongside antivirus, a password manager, and cloud backup.

That bundling is the single most important thing to understand. If you're already paying for Norton 360 Deluxe, you already have the VPN — there's no reason to buy it separately. If you're not, the standalone price is noticeably less attractive.

Norton's privacy policy states that it doesn't log browsing activity, and Deloitte audited that claim back in 2021. It's not a fresh audit, and the company is US-based (Five Eyes jurisdiction), but an audited no-logs statement from a known firm still beats an unaudited promise from a company you've never heard of.

OrionVPN

OrionVPN is a smaller provider aimed squarely at price-sensitive buyers. It advertises WireGuard and OpenVPN support, a kill switch on desktop, split tunneling, and a 30-day refund window. The marketing leans hard on the monthly cost, which is genuinely low — we're talking sub-$3 per month on multi-year plans.

Here's the honest caveat: a lot of OrionVPN's claims come from OrionVPN. We couldn't find an independent security audit, the company's jurisdiction isn't clearly spelled out on the site, and the server list is small enough that you'll notice. None of that makes it a scam. It does mean you're extending trust rather than verifying it, and that's a different transaction than buying from an audited provider.

Feature breakdown

Server network and locations

Norton runs roughly 2,000 servers across 30+ countries. That's mid-tier — not the sprawling 100-country networks you get from the biggest names, but enough that you'll usually find a nearby endpoint. Coverage is strongest in North America and Europe, thinner across Africa and parts of Asia.

OrionVPN's network is much smaller. The site lists somewhere in the range of 30 locations across roughly 20 countries, with heavy concentration in Europe and the US. That's fine if you only need a handful of regions. It's a problem if you need a specific country for streaming or for work.

Winner: Norton, on raw footprint.

Speed and protocols

Norton uses a proprietary tunneling protocol that it manages for you — you don't pick between WireGuard, OpenVPN, and IKEv2, because there's no protocol menu. That's convenient if you don't care and frustrating if you do. In practice, speeds are solid: on a 500 Mbps line we'd typically see 250–350 Mbps down through nearby servers, dropping to 100–150 Mbps on distant ones. That's plenty for 4K streaming and everyday work.

OrionVPN exposes WireGuard, OpenVPN, and IKEv2, which is the right call for a smaller provider — WireGuard is fast, and being able to fall back to OpenVPN over TCP when a network blocks UDP is genuinely useful. Peak throughput on nearby WireGuard servers was comparable to Norton in testing. Long-distance routes were less consistent, which is typical when a provider has fewer servers to route through.

Winner: tie, with a small nod to OrionVPN for giving you protocol control.

Privacy, logging, and audits

This is where the two diverge sharply.

Norton: no-logs policy, Deloitte-audited in 2021, US jurisdiction. The audit is dated, and US jurisdiction means a legal framework you may not love. But the policy is documented and has been checked by someone outside the company.
