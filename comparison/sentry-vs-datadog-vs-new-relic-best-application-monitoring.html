Sentry vs Datadog vs New Relic: Best Application Monitoring in 2026

Most teams don't pick a monitoring tool. They inherit one, or they sign up for whatever a senior engineer used at their last job, and then they live with it for three years.

That's a bad way to spend $15,000 a year. Sentry, Datadog, and New Relic overlap enough that the marketing pages look nearly identical, but they're built around different assumptions about how you work. Sentry assumes a developer is going to open a stack trace and fix something. Datadog assumes you have infrastructure to correlate. New Relic assumes you have a finance department that wants predictable line items.

I've run all three in production, and I've watched teams regret all three. Here's what actually separates them.

The short answer

  • Pick Sentry if your main pain is "something broke in production and I need to know exactly where." It's the best error tracking tool on the market, and it's cheap at small scale.
  • Pick Datadog if you run real infrastructure — containers, queues, databases, third-party services — and need everything in one place with correlation between metrics, traces, and logs.
  • Pick New Relic if you have a large engineering org and want per-seat pricing that doesn't explode when traffic doubles.

A solo developer or a five-person startup choosing Datadog is overbuying. A 60-person company with 400 microservices choosing Sentry as its only observability tool is going to have blind spots. That's the whole decision, roughly.

How I evaluated these

I looked at four things: how fast you get a useful answer out of the tool, what it costs at three realistic scales (indie, small team, mid-size org), how much of your stack it actually covers, and how much work it takes to keep it running. Pricing figures below are list prices as of early 2026, billed annually where a discount exists. Vendors change these constantly — verify before you commit.

Error tracking: Sentry's home turf

Sentry started as an exception tracker and never stopped being one. When an error fires, you get the stack trace, the breadcrumbs leading up to it, the release it shipped in, the user who hit it, and a link to the commit that probably caused it. There's a "suspect commits" feature that diffs your releases and points at the change that introduced the bug.

That workflow is the reason people stay. The issue detail page is dense but it's organized around one question: what do I change in the code?

Datadog's Error Tracking does the same job and has improved a lot, but it lives inside a much bigger product. Getting to the stack trace means navigating a service catalog first. It's fine if you already live in Datadog. It's friction if you don't.

New Relic's error tracking is competent and tied into NRQL queries, which is powerful once you know NRQL. The out-of-the-box experience assumes you'll build the view yourself.

Sentry also added Seer, its AI agent that attempts to root-cause an issue and propose a fix. It's genuinely useful on well-instrumented codebases and confidently wrong on messy ones. Treat it as a first draft, not an autopilot.

APM and distributed tracing

This is where the ranking flips.

Datadog has the most complete APM product of the three. Service maps, trace search, span-level flame graphs, and — critically — the ability to jump from a slow trace to the exact log lines and host metrics from that request. That correlation is the thing Datadog is actually selling, and it works.

New Relic has a mature APM with distributed tracing and a service map that's arguably cleaner than Datadog's. NRQL lets you query anything, which is a real advantage when you need a weird custom metric. The tradeoff is that New Relic's UI has accumulated a decade of navigation debt; you'll click through three menus to find something Datadog surfaces in one.

Sentry does tracing via spans, and it's good for what it is: you can see a slow database query inside a transaction and connect it to the error that followed. But Sentry doesn't Monitor your infrastructure. It won't tell you the Postgres replica is out of connections or that the queue depth is climbing. If your incident starts in the infrastructure layer, Sentry is the wrong tool.

Logs are the most expensive part of observability and the place where bills go wrong. Datadog charges $0.10/GB to ingest and then per million events to index, with the price depending on how long you keep them. Indexing everything is how you end up with a $9,000 monthly invoice. The fix is to index 10% of your logs and archive the rest — which is documented, but nobody reads the docs until after the bill.

New Relic includes 100 GB of ingest per month on the free tier and charges around $0.35/GB beyond it. For a team generating 400 GB, that's roughly $105 in overage. Much easier to predict.

Sentry's logs are a newer addition and are best understood as "context for errors" rather than a log platform. Don't buy Sentry expecting to replace Splunk.

Frontend and mobile

Sentry wins here, and it's not especially close for mobile. React Native, iOS, and Android crash reporting with symbolication, session replay, and release health tracking out of the box. If you ship a mobile app, this alone justifies Sentry.

Datadog RUM is strong for web — Core Web Vitals, session replay, funnel analysis — and it ties into your backend traces, which Sentry can't match. New Relic's browser monitoring is fine and frequently bundled.

Worked example — 8 engineers, 20 hosts, 500 GB logs/month, roughly 2M errors/month:

  • Sentry Business: around $300–450/mo depending on event volume.
  • Datadog: 20 hosts × ($15 infra + $31 APM) = $920, plus log ingest and indexing, lands around $1,300–1,600/mo.
  • New Relic: 8 users × $49 = $392, plus ~$140 in ingest overage, roughly $530/mo.

Datadog is 3x the cost of New Relic in that scenario and it's not 3x the product. It's the better product for infrastructure-heavy shops, but the premium is real and it compounds as you add hosts.

The counterargument: Datadog's per-host model means adding engineers costs nothing. New Relic's per-seat model means a 50-person team pays $2,450/mo before ingesting a single byte. Above roughly 30 engineers, Datadog's math starts looking reasonable again. Do the arithmetic for your actual headcount before you sign anything.

Pros and cons

Sentry

Pros

  • Best-in-class error tracking with commit-level context
  • Unlimited users on paid plans — no penalty for giving everyone access
  • Excellent mobile and frontend crash reporting
  • Cheap at small scale; free tier is genuinely usable
  • Session replay, profiling, cron monitoring, and uptime checks in one bill

Cons

  • Not a full observability platform — no infrastructure metrics
  • Log management is thin compared to the alternatives
  • Tracing is solid but shallower than Datadog's
  • Costs climb fast at high error volume; noisy codebases get expensive

Datadog

Pros

  • Broadest coverage: infra, APM, logs, RUM, synthetics, security, on-call
  • Correlation between metrics, traces, and logs is the best available
  • 800+ integrations, including almost anything you'd deploy
  • Datadog On-Call removes the need for a separate PagerDuty contract
  • LLM observability for teams shipping AI features

Cons

  • Expensive, and the bill is hard to predict before you've run it a month
  • Per-host pricing punishes autoscaling and ephemeral containers
  • Configuration is a project, not an afternoon
  • Easy to accidentally index far more logs than you meant to

  • Per-user pricing is predictable for large teams
  • NRQL is a genuinely powerful query language
  • Solid APM with a clean service map
  • Bundles alerting and incident intelligence without extra line items
