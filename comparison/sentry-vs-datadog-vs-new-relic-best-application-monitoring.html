Sentry vs Datadog vs New Relic: Best Application Monitoring in 2026

It's 2:14am and your phone is buzzing. A deploy went out four hours ago and something is throwing errors in production. You open your laptop and face the same question every developer faces eventually: where do I actually look?

Sentry, Datadog, and New Relic all want to be that answer. They're the three names that come up in every "what should we use for monitoring" thread, and they've been circling each other for years. But they're not interchangeable. They were built with different assumptions about who you are and what you're trying to find.

This comparison is written for solo developers, indie hackers, and small teams — the people who don't have a dedicated observability department. I'll cover what each tool actually does well, where it gets expensive, and which one I'd pick for different situations. Prices are list prices in USD as of early 2026, billed annually where noted. Vendors change these numbers constantly, so treat them as a starting point and verify before you commit.

The short version

If you want the answer before the details: Sentry is the best default for solo developers and small teams in 2026. It's focused on the thing you'll actually care about most days — errors and exceptions — and its pricing scales down to almost nothing when you're small. Datadog is the most complete platform and the right call once you have real infrastructure to Monitor, but its billing model will punish you if you're not paying attention. New Relic sits in the middle and is genuinely the value pick for teams with many hosts and few engineers.

Now let's look at why.

What each tool is actually built for

Sentry started as an error tracker and grew outward. Its core job is capturing exceptions, grouping them into issues, and telling you which release introduced them. Around that core it added tracing, session replay, profiling, and cron monitoring. It's an application monitoring tool in the sense that it watches your code, not your servers.

Datadog started from the infrastructure side and grew inward. It began as a metrics and dashboards platform for servers, then added APM, logs, real user monitoring, synthetic checks, CI visibility, and a pile of security products. It's a broad platform that happens to include application monitoring.

New Relic is the oldest of the three and has been through the most reinvention. Today it's a full-stack observability platform with a per-user pricing model that includes a data ingest allowance. It covers APM, browser and mobile monitoring, infrastructure, logs, and synthetic checks, all queried through NRQL.

That difference in origin matters more than any feature checklist. It shapes what each tool is good at and what it makes you pay for.

Feature breakdown

Error tracking and issue management

This is Sentry's home turf and it shows. Exceptions get grouped by stack trace and fingerprint, deduplicated across releases, and linked to the commit and pull request that introduced them. You get breadcrumbs — the trail of user actions, network calls, and console logs leading up to the error — attached to every event. Source maps for JavaScript and TypeScript are handled properly, including uploading them in CI so stack traces point at your actual source lines instead of minified bundles.

The workflow around issues is where Sentry pulls ahead. You can assign issues, mark them as resolved in the next release, and get an alert if a "resolved" issue comes back.
