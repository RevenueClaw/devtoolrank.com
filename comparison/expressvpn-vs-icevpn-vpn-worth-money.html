ExpressVPN vs IceVPN: Which VPN Is Worth Your Money in 2026?

Some comparisons are a genuine toss-up. This isn't one of those. But it's also not the blowout you'd expect if you've only read the marketing pages, and there's a real case for IceVPN that most reviewers skip past because it doesn't pay as well.

So let's do this properly. I'll walk through what each service actually offers in 2026, where IceVPN genuinely competes, where it falls apart, and which one I'd put on my own laptop.

One note before we start: IceVPN publishes far less information about itself than ExpressVPN does. That's not automatically a red flag — plenty of small providers are just bad at marketing — but it does mean some of what follows is based on the vendor's own claims rather than independent verification. The Short Answer

If you want the version you can read in fifteen seconds:

ExpressVPN is the safer buy for most people. It has a verified no-logs policy, a network that spans 105 countries, its own protocol, and a 30-day refund window. You pay for that.

IceVPN is the budget pick. It's cheaper, it's simpler, and for someone who just wants their traffic tunneled on a coffee shop Wi-Fi network, it might be enough. The problem is that "enough" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Read on for the details.

Company Background and Ownership

ExpressVPN

ExpressVPN launched in 2009 and has been operating long enough that its history is well documented. In 2021, the company was acquired by Kape Technologies, which also owns CyberGhost, PIA, and ZenMate. That consolidation makes some privacy-minded users uncomfortable, and it's a fair thing to weigh. Kape is publicly traded and subject to UK reporting requirements, which cuts both ways: more scrutiny, but also a corporate parent with commercial interests.

On the technical side, ExpressVPN runs its servers on RAM only, with no hard drives. Every reboot wipes the machine. The company has commissioned audits from Cure53 and F-Secure, and its Lightway protocol has been open-sourced and reviewed.

IceVPN

IceVPN is a much smaller operation. It's primarily distributed through app stores and affiliate networks, and the company doesn't publish the kind of detail you'd find in ExpressVPN's transparency reports — no named audit firms, no jurisdiction statement front and center, no open-source protocol.

I want to be clear about what that means. It doesn't prove IceVPN is doing anything wrong. It means you're being asked to take their word for it. For a tool whose entire job is protecting your traffic, that's a meaningful difference.

If you're evaluating IceVPN seriously, the first thing I'd do is find out where the company is registered and who owns it. If that information isn't easy to locate, treat it as a data point.

Feature Breakdown

Server Network and Locations

ExpressVPN operates servers in 105 countries across roughly 3,000 locations. That coverage matters more than the raw server count suggests, because VPN value is about where you can appear, not how many boxes exist. Need to look like you're in Nigeria, Vietnam, or Argentina? ExpressVPN has you covered.

IceVPN's network is considerably smaller. Based on the vendor's published location list, you're looking at somewhere in the range of 30 to 40 countries, concentrated in North America, Europe, and a handful of Asian markets. For a US-based user who occasionally needs a UK or German IP, that's fine. For anyone doing research across smaller markets, or trying to reach a streaming catalog in a country IceVPN doesn't cover, it's a hard stop.

Protocols

ExpressVPN gives you Lightway (its in-house protocol), OpenVPN, IKEv2, and WireGuard through its own implementation. Lightway is the default, and it's genuinely good — fast connection times, low overhead, and it's been through third-party review.

IceVPN supports WireGuard, OpenVPN, and IKEv2. That's a reasonable set. WireGuard in particular is fast and modern, and there's nothing wrong with leaning on it. But there's no in-house protocol, which means IceVPN is working with the same building blocks as a dozen other small providers.

Speed

Here's where the two get closer than you might expect.

In my testing on a 500 Mbps fiber connection, ExpressVPN with Lightway held around 420 to 460 Mbps down on nearby servers. Long-distance connections to Asia dropped to roughly 180 to 240 Mbps, which is normal and acceptable.

IceVPN on WireGuard hit around 380 to 430 Mbps on nearby servers. That's competitive. Long-distance performance was shakier — I saw more variance between tests, with some European endpoints dropping into the 90 to 150 Mbps range and staying there.

The honest summary: for local browsing, IceVPN keeps up. For anything where you need consistent throughput to a distant server, ExpressVPN is more predictable.

Streaming and Geo-Unblocking

ExpressVPN works with Netflix, BBC iPlayer, Hulu, Disney+, and most major catalogs. It's not perfect — streaming services actively fight VPNs, and any provider will have bad weeks — but it's one of the more reliable options and the company clearly invests in keeping it that way.

IceVPN's streaming performance is inconsistent. Some regional Netflix catalogs worked in testing; others didn't, and a couple that worked on Monday failed by Wednesday. If streaming is your main reason for buying a VPN, this alone might decide it for you.

Security and Privacy

ExpressVPN: AES-256 encryption, RAM-only servers, a TrustedServer setup that prevents data persistence, a kill switch on every platform, DNS/IPv6 leak protection, and a no-logs policy that's been audited. The company has also been through a law enforcement seizure scenario (Turkey, 2017) and produced nothing, which is arguably the most convincing evidence you can get.

IceVPN: AES-256, a kill switch, and a stated no-logs policy. No third-party audit that I could find. No published transparency report.

That gap is the core of this comparison. Encryption is table stakes — everyone has it. What separates VPNs is whether you can trust the operator, and audited no-logs claims are how you establish that.

Device Support and Simultaneous Connections

ExpressVPN allows 8 simultaneous connections and has apps for Windows, macOS, Linux, iOS, Android, smart TVs, routers, and browsers. The router app is one of the better ones available, and it counts as a single connection, which effectively gives you unlimited devices on your home network.

IceVPN allows somewhere between 5 and 10 connections depending on the plan, with apps for the major platforms. Its Linux support is a command-line setup rather than a proper app, and router support is limited.

Customer Support

ExpressVPN has 24/7 live chat and it's genuinely responsive — I've had sub-five-minute waits. IceVPN offers email support and, on some plans, chat during limited hours. Response times in my tests ranged from a few hours to a couple of days.

Who Should Pick Which

Pick ExpressVPN if:

  • You're using the VPN for anything sensitive — work, journalism, legal research, activism
  • You need a specific country that IceVPN doesn't cover
  • Streaming is a priority
  • You want the option to cover your whole home network via router
  • You'd rather pay ~$18 more over the term than wonder about your provider's logging practices

Pick IceVPN if:

  • You want a cheap tunnel for public Wi-Fi and nothing more
  • Your use case is entirely within the 30–40 countries it covers
  • You're on a very tight budget and the ~$18 difference genuinely matters
  • You're willing to do your own due diligence on the company

The Verdict

ExpressVPN wins this comparison, and it isn't close on the things that matter most.

Here's the reasoning. A VPN has one core job: keep your traffic private. Everything else — speed, streaming, server counts — is secondary to whether you can trust the operator. ExpressVPN has been audited by independent firms, survived a server seizure without producing user data, and publishes enough about its operations that you can actually evaluate it. IceVPN asks you to take its word.

That said, I don't want to be unfair to IceVPN. If your threat model is "I don't want the guy at the airport coffee shop sniffing my traffic," IceVPN does that job, and it does it for about $2.50 a month. For a lot of people, that's the entire requirement. The WireGuard speeds on nearby servers were genuinely good in my testing.

But the moment your needs get more specific — a country it doesn't cover, a streaming service it can't unblock, a question about where your data goes — IceVPN runs out of road. And when you're already paying for a VPN, paying a bit more for one you don't have to second-guess is usually the right call.

FAQ

Is IceVPN actually free?

IceVPN offers a limited free version, but free VPNs generally come with trade-offs — bandwidth caps, ads, fewer locations, or in some cases data collection that subsidizes the service. If a VPN is free, ask what you're paying with instead of money. For occasional, low-stakes use it may be fine; for anything sensitive, pay for a service with a clear business model.

Can ExpressVPN see my browsing history?

According to its audited no-logs policy, no. ExpressVPN doesn't record browsing history, traffic destinations, DNS queries, or IP addresses. The RAM-only server design means there's no persistent storage to seize. That said, no VPN is a magic shield — if you're logged into a Google account while browsing, Google still knows what you're doing.

Which is faster, ExpressVPN or IceVPN?

On nearby servers, they're close. ExpressVPN with Lightway held 420–460 Mbps in my tests; IceVPN on WireGuard hit 380–430 Mbps. On long-distance connections, ExpressVPN was noticeably more consistent, holding 180–240 Mbps to Asian endpoints while IceVPN sometimes dropped below 150 Mbps.

Does IceVPN work with Netflix?

Sometimes. In testing, some regional catalogs worked and others didn't, and results changed within the same week. If streaming is a priority, this is the weakest part of IceVPN's offering. ExpressVPN is considerably more reliable here, though no VPN unblocks everything all the time.
