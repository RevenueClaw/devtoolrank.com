Migrating from ExpressVPN to IceVPN in 2026: The Step-by-Step Switch Guide

The pitch for switching is easy to understand. ExpressVPN's 2-year deal bills $78.12 for 28 months (~$2.79/month effective). IceVPN's two-year intro rate is $2.49/month. Same basic job, modest price gap, right?

Before the walkthrough, read our full ExpressVPN vs IceVPN comparison for the head-to-head that motivates this switch.

Not exactly — but it's not nothing either. Let me put the actual numbers on the table before we get into the migration steps, because the decision you Make in the next ten minutes should be based on ~$18, not on a marketing headline.

Here's the short version: over a two-year term, ExpressVPN costs $78.12 (28 months billed upfront, ~$2.79/mo effective) and IceVPN costs $59.76. That's a ~$18 difference — about 75 cents a month. The bigger lever is renewal: ExpressVPN resets at $99.95/yr after the intro term.

Whether that $1 is worth it depends entirely on what you're doing with the VPN. This guide walks through the decision, then the actual switch — including the parts most "how to switch VPNs" posts skip, like the renewal trap at the end of the intro term and the devices you'll forget to migrate.

First: should you actually switch?

Before you cancel anything, answer one question honestly.

What is the VPN for?

If the answer is "I don't want the guy at the airport coffee shop sniffing my traffic," IceVPN does that job, and it does it for roughly $2.50 a month. If the answer is "I work on sensitive client data from hotels, I occasionally need a jurisdiction I can point to, and I want a provider whose no-logs claim has been independently audited," the ~$18 you save is not worth the uncertainty.

That's not a knock on IceVPN specifically. It's a statement about what you're buying when you buy a VPN. You're buying a trust claim. Everything else — the app, the protocol, the server list — is secondary, because the whole product proposition is "we can't see what you're doing." The cheap tier of the market simply has less public documentation behind that claim.

Our rule of thumb, and it holds up across the whole category: if you'd rather pay ~$18 more over the term than wonder about your provider's logging practices, stay put. If you're on a genuinely tight budget and the ~$18 matters, switching is reasonable — but do the migration properly, and set the renewal reminder on day one.

Two things worth flagging.

ExpressVPN's monthly plan is priced like a penalty. $12.99/month is $155.88 over a year — while the 2-year deal costs $78.12 for 28 months — more than the 12-month plan plus a nice dinner. If you're staying with ExpressVPN, never stay on monthly.

IceVPN's headline rate is an intro rate. Every IceVPN tier listed renews higher when the term ends. That's the same structure you see in hosting — the number in the ad is the acquisition price, not the steady-state price. Factor the renewal in before you commit to a long term.

And one practical note on that $4.99 IceVPN tier: the term it maps to isn't consistently labeled across resellers. If the 24-month $2.49 rate isn't showing at checkout, the $4.99 tier is the fallback, and at that point you're paying ExpressVPN's 24-month rate for a provider with less documentation. Run the numbers again before you click.

The two-year renewal math (do this before you switch)

This is the section that decides the whole thing, so let's be precise.

ExpressVPN, 2-year deal: $78.12 billed upfront for 28 months (~$2.79/mo effective), renewing at $99.95/yr. When the term ends, it renews at whatever the standard rate is at that time. There's no locked lifetime rate. Historically that means the second term costs more than the first unless you re-shop the deal.

IceVPN, 24 months: $2.49 × 24 = $59.76 at the intro rate. When that term ends, it renews higher — you won't know by how much until renewal time, which is exactly why you set a calendar reminder.

So on the first two-year term, you save ~$18 on the intro term. On the second term, the gap narrows, because both providers reset upward and IceVPN's reset is proportionally larger. If IceVPN renews anywhere near $3, you're at rough parity with ExpressVPN's intro rate and the upfront saving evaporates — which is why the renewal reminder matters.

That's the honest math. ~$18 over the term, plus a $99.95/yr renewal reset on the ExpressVPN side. If an $18 one-time saving is decisive for your budget, switch. If it isn't — and for most working developers it isn't — the price argument is weaker than the marketing suggests.

There's a third path people forget: downgrade your ExpressVPN term. Going from monthly to the 2-year deal costs $155.88 a year versus $78.12 for 28 months — roughly $120/year back in your pocket for nothing but a commitment. Feature breakdown: what you keep and what you give up

Let's go through the things that actually matter day to day.

Protocol and speed. ExpressVPN runs Lightway, its in-house protocol, alongside OpenVPN and WireGuard options. In practice Lightway is fast on reconnect and holds up on mobile networks where WireGuard sometimes stumbles. IceVPN's tiers are built around standard WireGuard-style tunnels. On a good connection you probably won't notice the difference. On hotel Wi-Fi with packet loss, you might.

Server footprint. ExpressVPN covers 105 countries. A bigger footprint matters less than you'd think — what matters is whether your specific country is covered. Check the list for the two or three places you actually connect through. If IceVPN covers them, the server count is irrelevant.

Independent audits. ExpressVPN has published multiple third-party audits of its no-logs claims, and runs RAM-only "TrustedServer" infrastructure, meaning servers don't write to disk. This is the single biggest actual difference between the two. For IceVPN, the public documentation is thinner — that's an observation, not an accusation. Before you commit to a two-year term, look for a published audit, a warrant canary, and a clear jurisdiction statement. If you can't find all three, don't buy 24 months. Buy one month, test, then decide.

Kill switch and leak protection. Both offer it. Test it yourself rather than trusting the toggle — step 6 below covers how.

Simultaneous devices. ExpressVPN allows 8. If you've got a laptop, phone, tablet, and a router, you're fine. Count your devices before you assume the cheaper provider's limit is generous enough.

Split tunneling. Available on both to varying degrees, and it's the feature you'll miss most if it's done badly. If you route your IDE's package manager traffic around the tunnel, verify that still works after migration.

Streaming and geo-testing. If you use the VPN to check how your site renders from another region, you need a provider that isn't constantly blocked by CDNs. ExpressVPN is generally reliable here. IceVPN is less proven — test the specific services you care about during the overlap window.

Extras. ExpressVPN bundles Threat Manager (blocker for known trackers and malicious domains) and sells the Aircove router for whole-network coverage. IceVPN's add-on story is thinner.

FAQ

Q: Is IceVPN actually cheaper over 2 years?

A: Yes on the first term — about $59.76 vs ExpressVPN's $78.12 (its 2-year deal runs 28 months at ~$2.79/mo effective). ~$18 apart, not $60.

Q: When should I stay on ExpressVPN?

A: If you depend on its streaming reliability or audited no-logs record and dislike re-testing apps.

The 2-Year Renewal Math (2026 Prices)

ExpressVPN 2-year true cost: $78.12 billed for 28 months (~$2.79/month effective), then it renews at $99.95/yr once the intro term ends — year three costs more than year one-and-a-bit did. IceVPN at $2.49/month intro x 24 = $59.76, but renewal rises off the intro rate — price year three before you commit. On first-term math IceVPN wins by ~$18; the renewal reset is the number to watch on both.

Note: this is a hosting-style annual-renewal market — promo entry prices are real, but the renewal rate is the number that matters over 24 months.

Verdict

IceVPN wins 2-year price; ExpressVPN wins on track record. Switch only if you'll actually re-test your workflows.
