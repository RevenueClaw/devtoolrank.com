Surfshark One for Homelab and Dev Use in 2026: Unlimited Devices Without the Upsell Maze

Most VPN reviews are written for people who want to watch Netflix from the wrong country. That's fine. But if you're reading this, you probably have a rack in the corner of your office, a Proxmox box, a Synology, a couple of Raspberry Pis, and a growing suspicion that your ISP is logging more than it lets on.

Surfshark's pitch fits that crowd better than most: one subscription, unlimited devices, no per-seat math. The question is whether the bundle — Surfshark One — actually holds up when you point it at a homelab instead of a laptop.

Short version: it does, for about 60% of what a homelab owner wants a VPN to do. The other 40% is where people get burned, and it's almost always about port forwarding.

Let's get into it.

What Surfshark One Actually Is in 2026

Surfshark sells three tiers, and the naming is genuinely confusing the first time you see it.

  • Starter — the VPN plus two small add-ons: Surfshark Search (a private search engine) and Surfshark Alert (breach monitoring).
  • One — everything in Starter, plus Surfshark Antivirus. This is the tier most people should look at.
  • One+ — everything in One, plus Incogni, Surfshark's automated data-broker removal service.

The thing worth internalizing: the VPN is identical in all three tiers. You are not buying a better VPN by upgrading. You're buying adjacent services. If you want the VPN and nothing else, Starter is the correct purchase, and anyone telling you otherwise is chasing a commission.

That "unlimited devices" line is the headline, and it's real. No device counting, no "family plan" upsell, no business tier you have to negotiate. One login covers everything you can authenticate.

Where Unlimited Devices Actually Earns Its Keep

On a two-laptop household, unlimited devices is a marketing bullet point. In a homelab, it changes what's practical.

Things a single Surfshark account covers without paying extra:

  • The primary dev laptop and a backup machine.
  • A Proxmox host running a handful of VMs, each of which needs egress through the tunnel when it's pulling distro ISOs or container images on a hotel network.
  • A Synology NAS doing offsite pulls.
  • Two Raspberry Pis — one as a DNS box, one as a monitoring node.
  • A travel router that holds a persistent WireGuard config.
  • Phone and tablet for testing mobile app behavior against geo-restricted endpoints.

Under a 5-device or 10-device cap, that list forces choices. You start rotating logins, sharing credentials with yourself on three devices at once, or dropping the Pi that matters because the laptop has priority. Unlimited removes a small but constant tax on how you build.

One honest caveat: "unlimited devices" is a technical allowance, not a license to run a hosting business. Surfshark's terms restrict commercial use and account sharing outside your household. Pointing forty ephemeral CI runners at a single tunnel is the kind of load that shows up in published rate limits, bandwidth caps, and aggregated user reports of throttling. Pointing twenty VMs at it for your own lab is normal and fine. Use judgment.

The Dev-Facing Features That Matter

WireGuard configs and the gluetun path

The most useful thing Surfshark does for a homelab is let you pull manual WireGuard credentials — private key, address, and per-server public keys — and drop them into anything that speaks WireGuard.

That means a gluetun container with your qBittorrent or *arr stack behind it, a router running OpenWrt, or a raw wg-quick interface on a Debian box. No client app, no GUI, no daemon to babysit. The tunnel comes up at boot and the healthcheck handles reconnects.

This is the workflow I'd recommend to anyone running an always-on tunnel in a lab. Client apps are for laptops. Containers want config files.

Linux support is CLI-only, and that's mostly fine

Surfshark ships a Linux app as a command-line interface rather than a desktop GUI. There's an official repo, package installs are straightforward, and you get surfshark-vpn connect style commands.

For a headless server this is actually preferable — no X11 dependency, no tray icon. For a Linux desktop user who wants split tunneling, it's a gap, because split tunneling isn't there on Linux. If you need selective routing on Linux, you're doing it with ip rule and wg-quick yourself, which is more work but more honest.

Rotating IP

Every time you reconnect, you can grab a new IP from the pool. Surfshark exposes this as a toggle. For scraping workflows, price aggregation scripts, or testing rate-limit behavior against third-party APIs, it removes a whole category of "why am I suddenly seeing 429s" debugging.

It's also useful for testing geo-dependent logic in your own apps without standing up a bunch of cloud VMs in different regions.
