DigitalOcean Review 2026: Still the Best VPS for Solo Developers?

DigitalOcean has been the default answer to "where do I put this thing?" for a decade. Slap down $6, get a Droplet, point your DNS at it, done. That pitch hasn't changed much. What has changed is everything around it — Hetzner undercutting everyone on raw price per core, Vultr spreading across more regions, Railway and Fly.io pulling people toward deploy-from-Git workflows.

So the honest question for 2026 isn't "is DigitalOcean good?" It's "is it still the right call for a solo developer or a two-person team who just needs to ship?"

I've run production workloads on all of these. Here's where DigitalOcean actually stands.

Short answer: yes, for most solo devs — but with two asterisks that matter, and I'll get to them.

What you're actually buying

DigitalOcean is a "developer cloud" in the sense that it packages compute, storage, networking, and managed services under one control plane with one bill. It's not trying to be AWS. It's trying to be the version of AWS that doesn't require a certification to operate.

The core product is the Droplet: a KVM virtual machine. You pick a plan, a region, an OS image (Ubuntu, Debian, Fedora, AlmaLinux, FreeBSD, Windows Server) or a one-click marketplace image, and it boots in about 55 seconds in my experience. Cloud-init is supported, which means you can hand it a YAML file and have your SSH keys, users, packages, and firewall rules set before the machine finishes its first boot. If you automate nothing else, automate that.

Billing is per-hour with a monthly cap. A $6 Droplet that runs 12 hours costs roughly 12 hours of $6/mo, not the full month. Small thing. Genuinely useful when you're spinning up throwaway environments.
